## 1. Choosing a style guide

The venue's own guide comes first. Otherwise use IEEE for engineering and computer science and APA for other academic writing. Do not mix the two systems in one document.

## 3. Abbreviations

- Write the full term first and the abbreviation in parentheses: posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD).
- Inside parentheses use brackets: (i.e., posttraumatic stress disorder [PTSD]).
- With a citation, separate by a semicolon: (CAPS-5; Weathers et al., 2018).
- Define again in the body even if the abstract defined it (APA and IEEE). Do not define abbreviations in headings.
- Use an abbreviation at least three times, otherwise write the term out. A standard abbreviation for a long, familiar term is the exception (APA).
- After defining, use only the abbreviation (APA).
- No definition needed for dictionary terms (AIDS, IQ), units (kg, cm), times with numbers (5 hr), Latin abbreviations (et al., i.e., e.g.), and common statistical symbols (M, SD, t) (APA).
- APA keeps Latin abbreviations inside parentheses. In running text write "for example" and "that is".

## 4. Punctuation

- Use the serial comma before the last item of three or more (APA).
- Use semicolons between list items that already contain commas (APA).
- En dash means "to", "through", or "and": pp. 10–15, 1984–1990, input–output, 10–20 cm. The em dash marks a break in thought (IEEE).
- Avoid piling several punctuation marks into one sentence. Split it instead (APA).
- No slash for "and/or". Write "X, Y, or both", or use a hyphen for paired terms: faculty-student ratio (PLAIN).
- Hyphenate compound modifiers before the noun only: a 42-foot-long boat, but the boat was 42 feet long (IEEE). Write prefixes closed: preprocessing, nonlinear, multiclass (APA).

## 5. Grammar and usage

- Agreement (IEEE): "A number of samples were taken", "The number N … is chosen", "The data were collected" (always plural), "A series of tests was run", "Three volts were applied".
- No contractions in technical text (IEEE).
- Verb tense (APA): past or present perfect for prior work ("Martin (2020) addressed"), past for methods and results ("Scores decreased"), present for implications ("The results indicate") and conclusions ("Limitations of the study are"). Keep one tense within a paragraph.
- Voice: APA permits both, and the checklist asks for the active voice as much as possible. PLAIN also favors the active voice.
- Anthropomorphism (APA): "The theory addresses", not "The theory concludes". Accepted constructions include "This paper focuses on", "The results suggest", "The study found", and "The data provide evidence that".
- First person (APA): use "I" or "we" for your own actions, avoid the editorial "we", and do not call yourself "the author". Venues and advisors may require otherwise.
- Capitalization: lowercase models, theories, and statistical procedures unless they contain a name (APA). Capitalize only the names attached to laws and theorems: Newton's first law (IEEE).
- Italics (APA): a key term at first use with its definition, titles of stand-alone works, statistical symbols and variables, scale anchors, and unfamiliar foreign words. Foreign-origin words in an English dictionary stay roman (a priori, per se, et al.). Avoid italics for emphasis.

## 6. Clarity (PLAIN)

- One idea per sentence. Break long sentences into parts.
- Keep subject, verb, and object close. Move insertions to their own sentence.
- Put modifiers next to the word they modify: "required to provide only the following", not "only required to provide the following".
- Put long conditions after the main clause.
- Uncover hidden verbs. They end in -ment, -tion, -sion, or -ance, often sit between "the" and "of", and pair with achieve, effect, give, have, make, reach, or take: "carry out a review of" → "review", "gain an understanding of" → "understand", "make an application" → "apply".
- Replace wordy phrases: a number of → several, a few, many; a sufficient number of → enough; at this point in time → now; is able to → can; on a monthly basis → monthly; on the ground that → because; in order to → to.
- Drop excess modifiers: absolutely, actually, completely, really, quite, totally. Drop redundant words: "worked together on a joint project" → "worked on a project".
- Use the same term for the same concept. Do not vary terms for style.
- Avoid double negatives and exceptions to exceptions.
- Minimize abbreviations. A short descriptive name is often clearer than an unfamiliar acronym.
- Use "must" for obligations and "should" for recommendations instead of "shall".

## 7. Spelling

- Use the first spelling in Merriam-Webster (APA and IEEE).
- IEEE changes British spellings to American: behaviour → behavior, centre → center.
- Keep one spelling system throughout.
